# What this project is about

Large language models develop a specific ability partway through training called an "induction head" — the skill of noticing that a chunk of text has appeared before and using that to predict what comes next. If a model has already seen "the cat sat on the ___" earlier in its context, an induction head lets it recognize that pattern the second time around and finish the phrase correctly, even for made-up words it's never seen in training. This one small mechanism is a big part of how language models do in-context learning at all — it's a well-studied, well-understood circuit, and it tends to switch on somewhat suddenly during training rather than easing in gradually.

We're using a small model, `pythia-70m`, and looking at the exact point in its training where this induction ability turns on. Our question is: once a model is sitting right before that switch flips, how small a change to its weights is actually needed to flip it? Not "how did the real training process happen to do it," but "what is the minimum amount of change required to install the behavior at all." And a second, more structural question: this circuit is really built from two cooperating parts — one that figures out *where* to look back in the text, and one that figures out *what* to copy forward from there. Do those two parts have fundamentally different mathematical character? Specifically, the "where to look" part is inherently about order (what came before vs. after), while the "what to copy" part isn't. We can measure that directly, and we predicted the two parts should look different by that measure.

# Why this matters

Understanding how a capability like this actually gets built inside a model — how much "room" it needs, and whether its two halves have different structure — is a small but concrete piece of a much bigger goal: understanding what's actually happening inside these models rather than treating them as black boxes. If a sophisticated behavior turns out to need only a tiny, low-dimensional nudge to appear, that tells us something about how compressible these capabilities are. If the two halves of the circuit really do have different mathematical signatures, that's evidence the model isn't just doing one generic thing — it's assembling genuinely distinct sub-mechanisms and gluing them together.

We were deliberately careful about what kind of claim we're allowed to make here. We are *not* trying to reverse-engineer exactly what happened during the model's real training run — that process is entangled with a huge amount of ordinary, general improvement that has nothing to do with induction specifically, so it's the wrong thing to study directly. Instead we start from a checkpoint of the model taken just before induction appears, and we try to *install* the behavior ourselves with a controlled, direct experiment. That gives us a clean, constructive answer to "how small a change suffices," even though it can't, by itself, tell us how the *original* training actually did it.

# Our plan

Before spending real time and compute on the main experiment, we set up a series of checkpoints ("gates") that each had to pass before we'd trust the next stage of work — the idea being that it's easy to run an experiment, get a discouraging number, and misread it as a deep finding when it's really just a broken setup or a bad hyperparameter. Each gate had a clear, decided-in-advance pass/fail line.

- First, we had to actually locate the moment induction turns on in this model's training and make sure it happens over a narrow window rather than a vague, spread-out region. It does — we found a "before" checkpoint and an "after" checkpoint just a few training steps apart.
- Second, we checked that a necessary building block already exists in the "before" model. Induction heads need to lean on a simpler, more primitive skill (noticing "the token right before this one"), and that simpler skill has to already be present for anything more sophisticated to be buildable on top of it. It was present, though not by a comfortable margin.
- Third, we measured how long training actually takes on the hardware we have (ordinary CPUs, deliberately — this is a small enough model that a GPU isn't needed and we wanted to keep costs down), so we'd know whether a full sweep of experiments was realistically affordable. It was.
- Fourth — and this is the one that didn't go as planned — we ran a "sanity check" experiment: before doing anything constrained or minimal, we asked whether training could install the behavior at all when given generous freedom to change the weights. This should have been the easy case. It failed. Whatever we changed, the trained model never developed working induction behavior.

Our working rules say that when a check like this fails, we don't get to just shrug and move on to the main experiment anyway, and we're also not allowed to quietly invent new tests on the fly to explain it away — any new diagnostic has to be a considered, deliberate decision, not something done reflexively. So a large part of the recent work has been carefully diagnosing *why* that sanity check failed, one hypothesis at a time.

# What we've found so far

We worked through a long list of ordinary explanations for the failure, and ruled almost all of them out one by one: it wasn't the learning rate (we tried many, and the model clearly does learn *something*, just not enough), it wasn't which specific part of the model we chose to focus the training on (we tried alternatives, and our original choice was actually the best of the bunch), it wasn't the specific weights we'd deliberately left frozen (letting them move too didn't help), and it wasn't a narrow technical constraint of the setup (removing every constraint and letting the *entire* model retrain still didn't produce the behavior).

The turning point was a different kind of test entirely. Rather than training anything, we directly copied pieces of the "after" model's real, already-working weights into the "before" model — no gradient descent at all. This tells us something training alone can't: if copying in the exact real solution for some piece of the model doesn't produce the behavior, then no amount of training could ever have found something better than that for the same piece, so the piece itself isn't where the missing ingredient lives. Copying in just the specific attention mechanism we'd been trying to train did essentially nothing. Copying in everything, unsurprisingly, worked completely — that just recreates the "after" model. But that left a wide, unexplained gap between "copying one small piece does nothing" and "copying literally everything works," with nothing in between ever having been tested.

We narrowed that gap in two ways. First, we found that the primitive building-block skill mentioned earlier (noticing the immediately preceding token) is present but dramatically weaker in the "before" model than in the "after" one — roughly a third as strong, sitting right at the edge of even counting as present at all. That's a believable single explanation for a lot of the difficulty: if that foundation is shaky, nothing built on top of it can work well no matter how it's trained.

Second, and most recently, we tested this directly by copying in increasingly large chunks of the network's early layers (the ones that come before the piece we'd originally focused on) and watching what happened. The result was clean and a little surprising: copying in those early layers almost completely restores the "where to look back in the text" half of the behavior — it goes from working essentially not at all to working almost as well as the fully-copied model. But the overall score, which also requires the "what to copy forward" half to work, barely moves at all, even though the specific attention mechanism responsible for that half was already using the real, working weights the whole time. In other words, we've now shown directly — not just argued — that these two halves of the circuit are genuinely separate problems. Fixing one doesn't fix the other, even when you'd expect it to.

# Where things stand now

We've made real progress narrowing down where the missing piece lives, but we haven't found it yet, and — deliberately — we haven't tried to guess our way to an answer. A recurring rule we're following throughout this project is that decisions about *which new experiment to try next*, or *how to interpret an ambiguous result*, are exactly the kind of judgment calls that should be made thoughtfully by a person rather than by an AI agent chasing the most interesting-looking thread on its own. That's a deliberate safeguard: this kind of investigation is exactly where it's easy to unconsciously go looking for a specific conclusion and find a way to manufacture it.

So the honest current state is: we know the "where to look back" half of the induction circuit can be almost fully restored by copying in the network's early layers, we know the "what to copy forward" half stubbornly cannot be restored that way even when its own dedicated weights are already correct, and we don't yet know where in the rest of the network that missing ingredient is hiding, or whether it's a single identifiable piece at all rather than something that only works when several pieces are put back together at once. The next concrete step is choosing which additional pieces of the network to test next — and that choice is waiting on deliberate human sign-off rather than being made automatically.
